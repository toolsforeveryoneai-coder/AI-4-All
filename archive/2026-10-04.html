A model Europe can keep behind its own walls

Edition of 2026-10-04

# AI For All ### Sunday, October 4, 2026

As of today, an IT lead at a European public administration or a regulated manufacturer can download a bilingual German-English model and run it entirely on machines it owns, so the data never leaves the building.

The model you are allowed to keep

Aleph Alpha released a new open model on Saturday, and it is selling custody rather than raw cleverness. Kolibri is bilingual by design, English and German, and it ships with its full weights, so a customer can download it and run it on its own hardware. The licence is Apache 2.0, which permits commercial use.

The company says it built the model in Germany and trained it on infrastructure in Germany and Finland, under European and German law, with no foreign control of the pipeline. It aims it at regulated work: public administration, industrials, aerospace. For a hospital group or a tax office that cannot send its data to a foreign cloud, that is the whole argument.

The run behind it was not smooth. Aleph Alpha says Kolibri took *21 days of pre-training, during which it hit 38 unplanned interruptions* that its pipeline repaired without a human stepping in. A model is only as sovereign as the team that can rebuild it, and the company is explicit that the durable asset is that team, not the release.

Weak signal

A senior safety employee at OpenAI, David Robinson, resigned and published an essay in The Atlantic arguing that the company's culture is broken and that safety failures will grow as its systems get more capable. The Guardian and TechCrunch both covered it on Saturday, and it was the most read AI story of the day.

It is worth noticing for reach, not for use. There is no product in it, no date, nothing a reader can do today, and the argument sits closer to a warning than to a fact you can check. Hold it next to OpenAI's recent decision to pause training and shelve a flagship release, and it reads as one more pressure point on the company the rest of the field takes its pace from.

The weekend's open-model news was not about beating the frontier. It was about who gets to keep the data, and a European lab has just made that a download.
