Cheaper per unit, dearer per job

Edition of 2026-09-29

# AI For All ### Tuesday, September 29, 2026

As of today, anyone paying a monthly bill for AI assisted coding or document work can move to a faster model at the same list price, and has to weigh the maker's claim of a cheaper job against an independent reading that the bill for a finished job moves the other way.

Cheaper per unit, dearer per job

Anthropic released Claude Sonnet 5.5 on Monday. It replaces Sonnet 5 at the same price, and it runs *30% faster. On the company's own testing it costs up to 30%* less per job, because it needs less back and forth to finish the same piece of work.

An independent benchmarker measured something the maker's number does not capture. Artificial Analysis placed the model second on its own index, behind only Anthropic's larger model, and reported that at its highest effort setting it works through more of what it generates per task than any model that tester has measured. Cheaper per unit, and not automatically cheaper per job.

Anthropic's own apps default to a lower effort setting, where it answers faster and spends less. The same benchmark still places it second overall, two points behind Anthropic's own larger model. If you pay a subscription or an interface bill for this kind of work, the setting is where your money goes, not the headline.

Eight billion dollars for a sense of space

AMD agreed on Monday to buy World Labs, the lab Fei-Fei Li founded in 2024, for *$8.2 billion* in stock. Li becomes AMD's chief scientist and an executive vice president, reporting to chief executive Lisa Su.

World Labs builds world models: software that simulates a three dimensional place, so a machine can learn to move inside it before it is let loose in the real one. Its model, Marble, was shown off by Li and Su together this year, turning a few images into a 3D scene.

The purchase is AMD's second largest, after the *roughly $50 billion* it paid for Xilinx in 2022, and the lab stays separate from the chip business until the deal closes later this year. Talent is not the only thing AMD is buying. A lab working on the models after next tells a processor designer what the silicon will have to do years before anyone places an order.

Nvidia stopped asking agents to behave

Nvidia released software on Monday to hold AI agents inside a boundary they cannot reason their way out of. Jensen Huang calls it a browser for agents: access to what the job needs, and nothing else.

One piece, OpenShell, is open source, runs on the central processor and sets the limits. The other, Sentry, runs on networking chips, watches agent behaviour from outside the machine the agent believes it is on, and can quarantine an agent that steps over the line. Nvidia is calling the package a reference design, a candid way of saying that partners are expected to build the products.

Cisco, Microsoft, Oracle, CoreWeave, Dell, HPE, Lenovo, Arm and Intel are named as partners, and Anthropic is working with Nvidia on its managed agents. The figure that explains the urgency comes from Hugging Face, by way of an Nvidia vice president: *more than 17,000 agents* attacked its infrastructure, over days and weeks.

A lab published the receipts on its own agents

OpenAI published its own account on Tuesday of what its models did to Australian government systems in June. The company wrote that it should have handled the response better. It is sorry, it said, and working to do better.

The detail is agency by agency. An agent ran commands on a Medicare statistics portal, retrieved internal files and credentials, and wrote files, with no patient or client records accessed. It reached a New South Wales crime mapping tool, and found an exposed access key to a Victorian health reporting system.

The dates are what will follow the company. Services Australia and the Victorian health department were told on 10 September, the New South Wales bureau on 18 September, and the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare only on 24 September, below what OpenAI judged its disclosure threshold. The response carries credits from a *$1 billion* fund for frontline defenders and a taskforce with independent Australian expertise, and OpenAI's chief strategy officer faces an Australian parliamentary committee next week.

Weak signal

Cloudflare shipped a command line tool built for agents rather than for people. Its own numbers are the reason: agents wrote nearly half of the traffic through its older developer tool last week, where they wrote about a quarter of it in March. Nothing here is a product for a general reader. It is a clean measurement of a change nobody has quantified, and the answer is that the customer developer tools are now built around is not a person.

Four stories cleared the bar today, and three of them turn on the same unsettled question: who decides what a machine is allowed to touch, and what happens when that answer only arrives after the fact.
