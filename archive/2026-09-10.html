Suno rebuilds its models on music it licensed

Edition of 2026-09-10

# AI For All ### Thursday, September 10, 2026

As of today, a working musician can generate and edit songs with a model Suno says was built from licensed catalogues rather than from the data its earlier models used, while the lawsuit over that older data is still running.

What people applaud is not what people run

Suno replaced its entire model line on Wednesday with a new family it says was rebuilt from scratch, trained on catalogues licensed from Warner Music Group, BMG and Believe rather than on the data behind its older models. For a producer who has kept the tool at arm's length for legal reasons, the offer is now a different offer.

The fast tier is free for everyone. The two larger models are for paying subscribers. The new abilities are practical: change one section of a song in plain language, pull a single instrument out and build a beat around it, start from a text, an image or a video. The old models were retired the same day.

Hold the applause. Universal and Sony, the two labels still suing Suno over the earlier data, are not part of the deal, and one publisher in a separate action is seeking damages that could approach one billion dollars. A cleaner tool now sits on top of a case that has not moved.

Your watch now keeps the last fifteen seconds

Apple showed the Apple Watch Series 12 on Wednesday, and the feature that will travel furthest is the audio. Press the crown and the watch shows the previous fifteen seconds of a conversation as text. A second feature, Siri Recap, writes up the key points of a chat once it is over.

Apple frames this as memory and accessibility, processed on the device and in its private cloud, and says the features arrive later this year. The watch itself is on pre-order now and ships later this month.

If you take confidential meetings, this is the sentence to sit with: a wrist across the table can hold the last few seconds of what you said. The software is not live yet, so nothing changes this morning. But the norm arrives before the update does, and it is worth deciding now how you feel about it.

One call, no answer, and the account falls

A research firm called Calif published a demonstration it calls WeWorm, a worm that spreads through WeChat calls on iPhone and Android. You do not answer. While the phone is still ringing, the attacker takes the account, and then the account calls your contacts to repeat the attack.

The flaw is a memory corruption in WeChat's call stack. Calif says it reported it to Tencent in July, and Tencent says the exploit is now mitigated for all users.

So there is nothing to patch this morning. The part to keep is the speed. Calif says it found the bug and wrote the exploit with AI in about two days, and the worm took one more week. Work that once needed a larger team and months now fits in a small one, which is a different kind of warning, and a more useful one than the usual panic.

The cart that fills itself

Instacart launched Clementine, an assistant that turns a sentence, a list or a recipe into a ready-to-buy cart. It is live now for most customers in the United States and Canada, at no extra cost.

It builds the order from the real-time shelves of the store you chose, not from an abstract catalogue. Instacart says orders placed with Clementine carry more items than its typical basket, past its one hundred fifteen dollar average.

For a weekly shopper this removes a chore, and that is the honest draw. It is also a quiet upsell: fifteen years of shopping data is what makes the suggestion feel personal, and personal is what makes it persuasive.

Weak signal

Ramp's monthly index of business AI spending, built on corporate card and bill-payment data, found adoption still rising but slowing. The striking part is at the top: the firms that spend the most on AI spent less per employee in August than they did in July. Ramp blames part of it on summer holidays and part on falling prices, which is a friendlier problem than demand collapsing. Watch whether the plateau holds once everyone is back at their desks.
