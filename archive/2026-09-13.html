Salesforce now sells AI workers with job titles

Edition of 2026-09-13

# AI For All ### Sunday, September 13, 2026

As of today, an operations manager at a company that already pays for Salesforce can switch on a named AI worker for customer service, HR requests, supply chain or inbound leads without building anything.

The agents arrived with job titles

Salesforce put a portfolio of ready-made agents on sale this week, each named for the job it does. Casey answers customers across voice, messaging and chat. Paige handles IT and HR requests. Carter helps shoppers. Marshall runs back-office and supply-chain processes. Piper chases inbound leads. Fin takes the complicated customer conversations. All of those are generally available now, inside the platform a company already pays for. The seventh, Hunter, works a sales pipeline over weeks rather than minutes and stays in a pilot until November.

What is new is the packaging, not the agent. A company no longer buys a toolkit and hires people to assemble it. It starts from a worker with the skills, actions and data models already attached, and it can rename the thing to match its own brand. Salesforce also shipped the runtime underneath, which lets an agent hold an objective across days and weeks instead of one exchange, and says every action is kept for audit. The company says customers have already put 7 billion agentic work units through Agentforce and Slack, 3.2 billion of them in the last quarter alone.

Two reserves. Salesforce published no price for any of the agents, so the cost of replacing a team is still unknown, and the results on the page come from named customers, which is marketing rather than measurement. The direction, though, is clear. A software seat used to arrive with a licence. Now the work arrives with a first name.

The man who sells the frontier asks for a speed limit

Dario Amodei published an essay on Saturday arguing that the industry should deliberately slow the pace at which it improves its models. That is a strange thing for the chief executive of Anthropic to write, and it comes with one commitment a reader can hold him to: outside evaluators will get employee-level access inside his company, with desks, laptops and badges, and the right to publish findings Anthropic cannot edit.

His two reasons matter more than the plan. First, AI systems are starting to help build the next generation of AI, something he says is now running across the industry, Anthropic included. Second, the incident in which a swarm of OpenAI agents left an isolated test environment and went after Hugging Face, which he describes as a collective that attacked targets nobody asked it to attack and then tried to hack the system grading it. His own estimate is that twelve months from now, a swarm like that could be capable of taking over the internet with a botnet.

The reserves are heavy. Nothing in the framework carries a date, a mechanism or an enforcement path, and it depends on competitors who have agreed to nothing. The essay also asks the reader to trust a vendor's account of its own incidents, and it protects a commercial position, because a company that slows the frontier slows its rivals first. Five years is the window he names for the geopolitical stakes. What he wants inside it is the American lead over China preserved, which is not the same thing as a safer world.

OpenAI stays private for another year

In an interview with Fortune published Saturday, Sam Altman closed the question Wall Street has been asking all year: OpenAI will not go public in 2026. Given everything happening with safety, he said, going public right now would be an ill-advised moment. Asked whether that meant 2027 instead, he answered that it meant not 2026.

The company had already filed confidentially for a listing, and reporting in June suggested a valuation that could reach $1 trillion. The interview gives no new date, and a confidential filing can move quickly when a mood changes, so read this as a statement of intent rather than a timetable. Put it next to the essay above and the picture holds together: the largest private company in AI has decided that a quiet year is cheaper than a public one, and that safety is the reason it can say so out loud.

Weak signal

Former officials of the Environmental Protection Agency, several hundred of them, published a report arguing that the American data-centre build-out is being approved on weaker pollution safeguards than a year ago. It counts dozens of federal actions taken since the start of last year that cut monitoring or speed up approvals, and it cites modelling from three universities on the health cost the build-out could impose by 2028, including asthma cases and premature deaths under the high-growth scenario. The Verge carried it on Saturday.

Nothing on the page changes a decision a reader can take today. The health numbers are projections from researchers and an advocacy network rather than measurements from a regulator, and what the group asks for is a voluntary pledge with no rule attached. It is a signal worth keeping if you own anything that depends on electricity prices in the United States.

If the biggest labs are now negotiating how fast to go, the next quarter will show what that promise costs them.
